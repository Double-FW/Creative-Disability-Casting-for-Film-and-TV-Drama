# **Overarching industry principles on disability portrayal in scripted drama and film**

Across all of these foundations, studios, broadcasters and industry bodies, several shared principles emerge that are directly relevant to casting and portrayal decisions in scripted drama and film:

* **Disability as core to diversity:** Disability is explicitly recognised as a central dimension of diversity, not an add‑on, and disabled people are framed as one of the largest minority groups whose stories, identities and contributions must be represented on and off screen.  
* **Authentic casting and lived experience:** There is a consistent expectation that disabled roles should be portrayed by disabled actors, with lived experience prioritised for disabled characters and, increasingly, disabled actors being considered for roles not written as disabled. Disabled literary and historical figures should be cast with disabled actors.  
* **Incidental and integrated portrayal, not just “issue” stories:** Disabled people should appear across genres and formats, including in incidental and integrated ways where their disability is not the sole focus of the narrative, alongside “core” disability‑led stories.  
* **Social Model and anti‑ableism:** Many commitments explicitly adopt or align with the Social Model of Disability, distinguishing impairments from disabling barriers, and reject ableist tropes such as “fixing” disabled people, pity or “cripping up”.  
* **Agency, 3D characters and valid identity:** Disabled characters and contributors should be portrayed as fully rounded people with agency, multiple identities, motivations, and storylines, with disability treated as a valid identity rather than a source of shame, pity or “inspiration porn.”  
* **Open, fair and inclusive casting processes:** There is a clear push for structural change in casting: open and fair auditions, specific commitments to audition disabled actors for new productions, and proactive searching for disabled performers for both disabled and non‑disabled roles.  
* **Structural access and workplace change:** Inclusion is framed not only as an on‑screen issue but as a systemic production issue, requiring accessible studios, sets, recruitment and working practices, with guidelines such as the “5 As” (Anticipate, Ask, Assess, Adjust, Advocate) to embed access.  
* **Measurement, targets and accountability:** Several initiatives include numerical targets (for representation and investment), annual reporting, code‑of‑practice compliance and use of standardised data tools (e.g., Diamond) to track progress on disability representation on and off screen.  
* **Leadership, governance and industry‑wide collaboration:** Disability inclusion is positioned as a leadership responsibility and a collective industry obligation, with cross‑industry projects (e.g., TAP, Doubling Disability), advisory groups and creative diversity roles driving alignment and shared standards.

These principles can be operationalised in casting processes as: always seeking disabled actors for disabled roles; routinely considering disabled actors for any role; ensuring auditions and workplaces are accessible.

# **The Ruderman Family Foundation commitments**

## **Authentic Casting Pledge (full text from source)**

The Ruderman Family Foundation's commitments to disability representation are primarily codified in its Authentic Casting Pledge and the Seal of Approval program. These initiatives target systemic exclusion in Hollywood by securing public commitments from major studios and networks.

The Authentic Casting Pledge

1. Major entertainment companies—including NBCUniversal, Sony Pictures Entertainment, Paramount, and CBS—have signed this pledge. Its core commitments include:  
* **Recognition of Diversity:** Acknowledging that disability is central to diversity and that the disability community is one of the largest minority groups in the U.S.  
* **Audit Commitment:** Pledging to audition actors with disabilities for each new production picked up to series or film.  
* **Systemic Inclusion:** Committing to increasing auditions for actors with disabilities regardless of the role size to foster long-term industry change.

Seal of Approval for Authentic Representation

2. The foundation awards this "Seal" to television and film projects that meet specific criteria for inclusive casting:  
* **Authentic Casting:** The production must feature actors with disabilities in roles that portray disabilities.  
* **Substantial Roles:** The actor must have a speaking role of at least five lines.  
* **Awardees:** Recipients include major productions like Grey's Anatomy, This Is Us, Atypical, See, and Out of My Mind.

As of 2025, the four major studios that have formally signed the pledge to audition actors with disabilities for all new productions are:

* CBS Entertainment  
* NBCUniversal  
* Paramount Pictures  
* Sony Pictures Entertainment

**Hulu and Other Streaming Platforms.** While Hulu hasn't signed the systemic pledge, it actively participates through the Seal of Authentic Representation program. This means individual Hulu productions are vetted and honored for their casting choices.

* **Hulu Honorees:** The series Ramy and the film Run both received the Seal for casting actors with disabilities in leading roles. Most recently, Only Murders in the Building was honored for casting deaf actor James Caverly.  
* **Other Platforms:** Netflix and Disney+ have also received multiple Seals of Approval for shows like Atypical, Hawkeye, and the 2024 film Out of My Mind.  
* **BBC Studios:** In 2022, BBC Studios launched its own "The Pledge" in alignment with Ruderman’s goals to ensure disability inclusion across all its productions.

The foundation continues to advocate for all major streaming services to move from project-by-project inclusion to the full studio-wide audition pledge.

These official announcements cover the major studios and streaming platforms that have joined initiatives to audition actors with disabilities.

  * Always recognise disability as a core diversity dimension when considering talent.  
  * For every new series or film, commit to auditioning actors with disabilities, not just for disabled‑labelled roles but across roles and role sizes.  
  * Treat increased audition volume for disabled actors as a structural, long‑term practice, not a one‑off gesture.

# **British Film Institute (BFI) approach to disability portrayal**

**Social Model of Disability:**  

* The BFI explicitly promotes the Social Model, understanding disability as the result of societal barriers (attitudes, inaccessible environments) rather than an individual “tragedy.” This reframes casting and storytelling: the problem to solve is not the disabled character, but the world around them.

**Identity‑first language and valid identity:**  

* The BFI encourages identity‑first language (“disabled people”) to affirm disability as a valid identity, while respecting how individuals choose to self-identify. Scripts and casting briefs should reflect this stance and avoid pathologising language.

**Anti‑ableism and anti‑tropes:**  

* The BFI takes an explicit anti‑ableism stance, opposing practices such as “cripping up” (non‑disabled actors playing disabled roles). For casting, this means always seeking disabled actors for disabled roles.

**Supporting disabled talent and authentic stories:**  

* Via structures like the Disability Advisory Group (DSAG), and tools such as Inclusive Talent agencies and [Profile Performers](https://profileperformers.com/) databases, the BFI helps productions find disabled talent. It also backs campaigns and curated collections that highlight authentic disability storytelling, with disabled creators leading the narrative.

**Accessibility plans and funded projects:**  

* BFI‑funded projects are required to have accessibility plans covering recruitment, set locations and production practices. For scripted drama and film, this commits productions to both accessible working environments and inclusive casting of disabled performers.

In casting contexts, the BFI approach can be translated into rules of thumb: treat disability as identity, not tragedy; avoid harmful tropes and “cripping up”; ensure disabled actors are considered first for disabled roles; and design productions around the needs and leadership of disabled creatives.

## **Channel 4 (and Film4) – Disability Code of Portrayal and casting**

### **Programme‑level Disability Code of Portrayal (relevant points)**

Channel 4’s Disability Code of Portrayal is designed “to ensure authentic, nuanced and original disability portrayal.” Key programme‑level commitments include:

* **Give disabled people agency:**    
  “In the spirit of ‘nothing about us, without us’, disabled people in our content have their own voice and tell their own stories wherever possible.”  
* **Show disabled people in 3D:**    
  Disabled characters are more than their conditions or impairments; they are well‑rounded with other identities and motivations.  
* **Represent being disabled as a valid identity:**    
  Identifying as disabled is not undesirable or shameful; it is a legitimate identity to own and be proud of.  
* **Portray disability as a complex, nuanced human experience:**    
  The diversity and complexity of disabled experiences should be reflected; simplification is to be avoided.  
* **Apply Social Model thinking:**    
  Clear distinction between impairments and the impacts of disabling environments, structures and attitudes.  
* **Remove the ‘able gaze’:**    
  Avoid assumptions and projections about disabled experience; check for non‑disabled bias, privilege or ignorance.  
* **Have empathy with, rather than sympathy for, disabled people:**    
  Avoid patronising, pitying or objectifying; instead seek understanding without claiming total knowledge.  
* **Seek disabled performers for disabled roles:**    
  In scripted content, “we look to cast people with similar conditions or impairments in disabled roles; we also seek to put disabled performers in roles not specifically written to be disabled.”  
* **Neither overplay nor underplay disability:**    
  Disability drives the story only when it genuinely needs to, but is not glossed over where relevant to lived experience.  
* **Say something insightful to disabled viewers:**    
  Disability content should be enlightening and engaging for both disabled and non‑disabled viewers, going beyond simple awareness‑raising.

### **Portfolio‑level commitments relevant to casting**

At portfolio level, Channel 4 commits to:

* **Range of portrayal types:**  
  * **Incidental** – disabled people included without direct comment.  
  * **Integrated** – disability addressed sometimes and not at others.  
  * **Core** – disabled people at the centre of the narrative.  
* **Favour inclusively disabled shows over exclusively disabled stories** to avoid “othering.”  
* **Offset stereotypes and tropes** that may be genre‑required.  
* **Ensure senior disabled editorial input** in core disability stories.  
* **Peer review portrayal** against tropes and stereotypes.

**Volume of representation:**    
“Every series will have at least one disabled performer, contributor, guest or presenter, with the intention for more than one,” with encouragement for similar ambition in singles.

**Summary for Channel 4 / Film4 casting in scripted drama and film**

* **Authentic roles:** Channel 4 explicitly states it will seek disabled performers for disabled roles, ideally with similar conditions/impairments/challenges, aligning with lived‑experience casting standards.  
* **Incidental portrayal:** There is a structural aim that every series includes at least one disabled performer, normalising disability as part of the ensemble, not only in disability‑centred narratives.  
* **Creative safeguards:** Removal of the “able gaze,” peer review for tropes, and senior disabled editorial input act as checks on how casting and scripts land for disabled audiences.

For Film4 and Channel 4 scripted commissioners and casting teams, the implication is: routinely embed disabled performers in ensemble and lead roles; treat lived‑experience casting as the norm; and scrutinise casting and story choices against the Disability Code of Portrayal and Social Model principles.

TAP is a pan‑industry alliance. It was created in response to Jack Thorne’s 2021 MacTaggart Lecture, which highlighted the “appalling lack of access, representation and provisions” for disabled people in the industry.

## **Focus of TAP with respect to scripted drama and film**

Although TAP spans scripted and unscripted content, key aspects directly relevant to scripted drama and film‑making include:

**Access on productions and in studios/facilities:**    
TAP addresses physical accessibility on productions and in studios, post‑production facilities and locations, recognising that disabled talent cannot contribute to scripted drama and film if spaces are inaccessible.

**5 As production guidelines for disability inclusion.** TAP has published guidelines committing adopters to:

1. **Anticipate:**  
   * Expect to work with disabled people regularly and ensure structures, processes and environments are proactively inclusive (“access first”).  
     * Have dedicated inclusion policies for recruitment, budgets, schedules; time and money should never be reasons for excluding disabled talent.  
     * Work with professionals and organisations with lived experience and specialist expertise; understand legal frameworks (e.g., Equality Act 2010\) and funding support (e.g., Access to Work).  
   2. **Ask:**  
      * Ensure accessible recruitment and casting processes, consistently offering adjustments and support.  
      * Focus on access and adjustments, not diagnoses.  
      * Regularly check‑in on adjustment effectiveness; use tools like Access/Disability Passports.  
      * Put disability inclusion on the agenda at every production stage, and track inclusion via tools like Diamond.  
      * Respect confidentiality and bring in Access Co‑ordinators early (commissioning, crewing, casting).  
   3. **Assess:**  
      * Produce accurate access statements (environments, communication methods, working patterns, disability knowledge).  
      * Communicate access clearly on websites and corporate material, including recruitment.  
      * Seek feedback from disabled talent; adjust schedules and spaces to support performance.  
   4. **Adjust:**  
      * Ensure all adjustments are in place before disabled talent begins.  
      * Secure funding in advance; provide flexible ways of working (remote, flexible hours, job shares).  
      * Appoint an “Access Contact” on each team and work with trained Access Co‑ordinators.  
      * Adjust for all professional contexts, from training to wrap parties.  
   5. **Advocate:**  
      * Clearly set expectations for disability inclusion and challenge ableism/disablism.  
      * Work with disabled talent because of the distinctive skills and perspectives they bring; guarantee equal pay for equal work.  
      * Support long‑term career progression for disabled people and act as advocates and allies across networks.

These standards are explicitly intended to be adopted by any broadcaster, streamer, production company or industry body working in TV and film, including scripted drama and feature films.

## **Alignment with the Creative Diversity Network and Doubling Disability**

**Use of Diamond data:**  

* The Creative Diversity Network (CDN) provides the Diamond data that originally underpinned the Doubling Disability initiative, which aimed to double disabled people in off‑screen roles from 4.5% to 9%. Diamond data has been used to evidence underrepresentation and now to track progress.  
* **Doubling Disability achievements (headline figures from text):**  
  * Goal: 4.5% to 9% disabled people working off‑screen, as per The First Cut (2018).  
  * By 2022/23–23/24:  
    * Off‑screen disability representation reached 9.6%.  
    * On‑screen contributions by disabled people increased from 8.0% to 8.9%, with later figures suggesting around 9.7%.  
  * The aim of reaching more than 9% representation has been achieved on both off‑screen and on‑screen metrics.

**CDN’s role in TAP:**    
CDN is described as a “data‑driven, evidence‑based organisation,” with Diamond data being “a core part of not just highlighting the problem but being part of the solution,” and disability established as part of the mainstream diversity conversation. TAP builds on this evidence base to drive structural change.

For scripted casting, TAP \+ CDN essentially set a standard that:

* Casting and production spaces must be designed with access in mind from the start (Anticipate), not retrofitted.  
* Disabled talent should be actively sought and supported at every stage, in front of and behind the camera.  
* Progress must be measured using consistent data, and disability representation should rise towards population parity.

It asks executive producers and senior production leaders to promote an inclusive and respectful working environment, uphold BBC values, respond appropriately to concerns and ensure that unacceptable behaviour is addressed.

Although this supports the conditions in which disabled performers and crew can work safely and equitably, it does not establish specific requirements for authentic disability casting, auditioning disabled actors or considering disabled performers for roles not written as disabled.

For policy analysis, “The Pledge” should therefore be treated as evidence of BBC Studios’ commitment to inclusive production culture rather than as an authentic portrayal or casting policy.

## **8\. Channel 4 – Disability Code of Portrayal**

[Disability Code of Portrayal Document](https://assets-corporate.channel4.com/_flysystem/s3/documents/2023-03/Channel4_Disability%20Code%20of%20Portrayal_July2022.pdf) 

**Summary:**    
Channel 4’s Disability Code of Portrayal provides detailed guidelines for authentic, nuanced and original disability portrayal. It mandates agency for disabled people (“nothing about us, without us”), 3D characters, disability as a valid identity, Social Model thinking, removal of the “able gaze,” and empathy rather than sympathy. It commits to seeking disabled performers for disabled roles and placing disabled performers in roles not written as disabled, while ensuring every series includes at least one disabled performer and deploying senior disabled editorial input and peer review to avoid stereotypes and tropes.

## **9\. TV Access Project (TAP) – 5 As guidelines**

[TV Access Project: 5 As guidance.](https://creativediversitynetwork.com/diversity-in-practice/tv-access-project/divesity-in-practice-tv-access-project-tv-access-project-the-5as/)

**Summary:**    
TAP is a cross‑industry alliance (BBC, Channel 4, BritBox International, Disney+ UK, ITV Studios, Paramount, Prime Video, Sky, UKTV, CDN, PACT and others) aimed at creating an inclusive TV production sector for disabled talent, focusing on access on productions and in studios/facilities. Its 5 As (Anticipate, Ask, Assess, Adjust, Advocate) set simple, cross‑industry guidelines for working with disabled talent and providing access requirements. For scripted drama and film, this translates into accessible casting processes, proactive inclusion and support for disabled performers and crew, and a culture in which disability inclusion is planned, resourced and championed.

## **10\. Creative Diversity Network (CDN) – Doubling Disability**

[Creative Diversity Network – Doubling Disability](https://creativediversitynetwork.com/doubling-disability/)

**Summary:**    
Doubling Disability, launched in 2018 by CDN with backing from major broadcasters (BBC, Channel 4, ITV Studios, Sky, Channel 5/Viacom, ITN, Pact and the DWP), set out to double disabled people in off‑screen roles from 4.5% to 9%. Extended beyond its initial two‑year window, the initiative reached its goal earlier than anticipated, with off‑screen representation at 9.6% and on‑screen contributions rising to around 9–10%.   
Diamond data underpins both the problem diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. The project embeds disability as a key metric of diversity across UK broadcasting, reinforcing that scripted drama and film must continually increase representation behind and in front of the camera.
