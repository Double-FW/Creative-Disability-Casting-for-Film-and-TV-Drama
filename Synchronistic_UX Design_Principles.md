# Synchronistic Design Principles

## A human-centred framework for aligning brand, product and experience

## Purpose

Good experiences rarely result from one design decision.

They emerge when the promise made by an organisation, the capability of its product, the behaviour of its interface and the reality of a person's situation come together at the right moment.

When that happens, the experience can feel almost synchronistic. The product seems to understand what the person is trying to do. Information appears when it becomes useful. Feedback makes sense. Different parts of the organisation behave as though they belong to the same system.

This is not coincidence. It is the result of deliberate alignment.

> **Synchronistic design aligns organisational promise, product capability, system behaviour and human context at the moments that matter.**

That alignment must not be reserved for an organisation's imagined default customer.

> **An equivalent experience ensures that meaningful alignment remains available across different ways of perceiving, understanding and interacting.**

The following principles are intended to help brand, product, customer experience, service, UX, inclusive design and accessibility teams work towards that shared outcome.

They are not a sequence of stages. They are questions that should remain active throughout research, design, development, delivery and evaluation.

---

## 1. Align the promise with the experience

What an organisation says should be supported by what its products and services actually do.

A promise of simplicity, independence, personalisation or inclusion creates an expectation. That expectation cannot be fulfilled by communications alone. It must be expressed through product behaviour, service decisions and customer outcomes.

If an organisation promises independence but some customers must ask for help to complete an ordinary task, the transaction may succeed while the promise fails.

Brand is not separate from experience. The experience is where the promise is tested.

**Ask:** Does the product behave in a way that makes the organisation's promise believable?

---

## 2. Design around human intention

People use products to achieve purposes. They do not use them because they want to complete a user flow.

A flow should support what someone is trying to accomplish rather than require them to follow the organisation's preferred route. People should be able to pause, reconsider, change direction and return without losing meaning or progress.

If successful use depends on behaving exactly as the design team expected, the experience is aligned with the process rather than the person.

**Ask:** Is the experience supporting the person's purpose or merely enforcing the system's route?

---

## 3. Respond at the moment of need

Information, feedback and assistance should appear when they become meaningful.

Information provided too early may be forgotten. Information provided too late may no longer help. A warning that interrupts someone before they understand its relevance can be as unhelpful as a warning that arrives after the decision has been made.

Timing must also work across different forms of interaction. Feedback that is immediate visually but delayed, incomplete or absent through another mode is not meaningfully synchronised.

**Ask:** Does the response arrive when this person can perceive, understand and use it?

---

## 4. Preserve orientation, not just movement

A successful flow does more than move someone forwards. It helps them remain oriented and in control.

People should be able to understand:

- where they are;
- what has happened;
- what they can do next;
- what consequences a decision may have;
- how to change, reverse or correct it.

Speed is not the same as flow. Removing steps is not automatically an improvement if the remaining journey becomes harder to understand. Sometimes a pause, explanation or confirmation is part of a good experience.

**Ask:** Can the person remain oriented and make informed choices throughout the journey?

---

## 5. Design for equivalent experience

Equivalent does not mean identical.

Different people may use different formats, interactions, technologies or service routes. The important question is whether the essential value of the experience survives those differences.

An equivalent experience should provide a comparable:

- purpose and outcome;
- level of information and understanding;
- degree of agency and choice;
- level of privacy and dignity;
- demand on time and effort;
- likelihood of success.

An alternative is not necessarily equivalent. “You can call us instead” may make a task technically possible, but it is not comparable if the main route is private and available at any time while the telephone route has restricted hours, takes longer or requires someone to explain why they need help.

Reaching the same administrative endpoint does not prove equivalence of experience.

**Ask:** Does the alternative preserve what matters, or does it merely make completion possible?

---

## 6. Make every change available

When the system changes, that change must be perceivable and understandable through the ways people are using it.

An address lookup may display suggestions at precisely the right moment for a sighted mouse user. If those suggestions cannot be reached with a keyboard or are not announced by a screen reader, the interface has not failed to respond. It has responded selectively.

The same principle applies beyond visual and nonvisual access. A message may appear but disappear before someone can process it. An interaction may change before a person understands what caused the change. An announcement may technically occur without providing enough context to be useful.

**Ask:** Who knows that something has changed, and who has been left in the previous state?

---

## 7. Anticipate without taking control

Prediction can reduce effort. It can also replace the person's intention with the system's guess.

Systems may suggest, adapt or prepare. They should not quietly remove choice or make assumptions difficult to recognise and correct. People need to understand important predictions, reject them and recover when they are wrong.

This becomes particularly important when AI shapes interfaces, content or customer journeys. Appearing to understand a person is not the same as understanding them. Sometimes the most respectful system behaviour is to ask.

Anticipation should be useful, not compulsory.

**Ask:** If the system anticipates incorrectly, can the person recognise and correct it?

---

## 8. Treat recovery as part of the experience

Errors, interruptions and changes of mind are ordinary human behaviour. They should not be treated as exceptional cases.

A synchronistic experience should survive:

- an incorrect selection;
- an expired session;
- a lost connection;
- an interrupted task;
- a change of device or channel;
- a need for additional support.

Recovery should preserve as much context, progress and dignity as possible. When the expected journey breaks, the relationship between the person and the organisation should not break with it.

**Ask:** Can the person recover without unnecessary loss of progress, confidence or control?

---

## 9. Design across organisational seams

Customers experience one organisation even when responsibility is divided between brand, product, UX, technology, service and accessibility teams.

The handovers between those areas are often where alignment fails. An interface may be accessible while the policy behind it is exclusionary. A digital channel may remember the customer while the telephone service asks them to begin again. Marketing may promise simplicity while identity checks create unnecessary complexity.

Specialist teams remain necessary. The aim is not to remove their distinct expertise. It is to prevent organisational boundaries from becoming barriers within the customer experience.

**Ask:** What must pass between teams and channels for the person's experience to remain continuous?

---

## 10. Measure where alignment breaks

Task completion, technical conformance and satisfaction scores are useful. None tells the whole story.

A person may complete a task while experiencing unnecessary effort, uncertainty or loss of independence. A product may meet defined technical requirements while failing to provide a comparable experience. A satisfaction score may record an answer without explaining where confidence was lost.

Look for the points at which people:

- lose orientation or confidence;
- repeat information;
- wait unnecessarily;
- change channels to obtain help;
- surrender privacy or independence;
- receive inconsistent information;
- abandon an otherwise possible task.

These points reveal where the organisation, product and interaction have fallen out of step with the person.

**Ask:** Where does alignment break, for whom, and what evidence would help us understand why?

---

## How the principles work together

The principles operate across three connected levels.

### Organisational alignment

The organisation's promise, decisions, policies and channels should support a coherent experience.

Relevant principles:

- Align the promise with the experience.
- Design across organisational seams.
- Measure where alignment breaks.

### Interaction alignment

The product should remain understandable, responsive and recoverable as someone moves through it.

Relevant principles:

- Design around human intention.
- Respond at the moment of need.
- Preserve orientation, not just movement.
- Make every change available.
- Treat recovery as part of the experience.

### Human equivalence

The value of the experience should remain meaningfully available across different people, contexts and forms of interaction.

Relevant principles:

- Design for equivalent experience.
- Anticipate without taking control.
- Measure where alignment breaks.

Inclusive design runs through all three levels. It is not a final review stage or an additional link in the chain. It continually tests whose intentions, circumstances and ways of interacting have been anticipated.

---

## A working proposition

> **Synchronistic design does not make people conform to a perfectly designed flow. It keeps the organisation, product and interaction in step with the person—even when that person pauses, changes direction, interacts differently or does something the system did not predict.**

The aim is not to make every experience identical. Nor is it to anticipate every possible human situation.

The aim is to maintain a meaningful relationship between what the organisation promises, what the product enables and what the person actually experiences.

When that relationship remains coherent across different people and contexts, good experience stops being an accident.
