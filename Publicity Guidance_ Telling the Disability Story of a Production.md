# Publicity Guidance: Telling the Disability Story of a Production

Creative, consent-led opportunities for press, marketing and talent teams.

The disability story is not an obligation to disclose. It is an opportunity to reveal the craft, relationships, choices and human experience that made the production more distinctive.

**Purpose.** This guide supports publicity departments working across film, television, streaming and theatre. It is designed to help teams discover richer stories around disabled and neurodivergent talent, while remaining consent-led, rights-respecting and grounded in the production’s creative purpose.

**Approach.** The guidance is deliberately opportunity-led rather than rule-led. It offers questions, story lenses, examples and practical tools that publicity teams can adapt to the production, the talent and the audience.

**Audience context.** Disabled people are not a niche audience. Current UK government statistics classify one in four people as disabled. The potential connection is wider still when families, partners, friends and colleagues are considered.

<a id="1-the-publicity-opportunity"></a>
# **1. The publicity opportunity**

Publicity around disability is often treated as a sensitive risk to manage or a representation claim to substantiate. That can make press materials cautious, generic and defensive. A more useful strategy is to treat the disability story as a creative opportunity: a chance to reveal the production’s craft, decision-making and emotional depth.

Casting guidance already recognises disability as a creative choice that can change how audiences read power, vulnerability, authority, attraction, danger and intimacy. Publicity can continue that creative emphasis.

The strongest publicity does not announce that a production is inclusive and then move on. It gives the audience evidence of the creative decisions, collaborations and performances that made the inclusion meaningful.

## **A story larger than representation**

A disabled actor may have an unusual route into the industry, but the publicity story is not simply that they were given an opportunity. It may be about the discipline they developed while navigating a difficult industry, or the creative process through which a production became more specific and more alive.

The production itself may also have a disability story: a familiar character understood differently, a relationship made more specific, a historical world made more truthful, an action sequence reimagined with better physical logic, or an ensemble made more recognisably human.

## **The size of the connection**

Disability can connect with disabled and neurodivergent viewers directly, but the emotional reach is broader. Families and loved ones may recognise negotiation, protection, independence, frustration, care, humour and identity.

This means a disability story can be both culturally significant and widely human. It does not need to be made universal by removing disability from it. Its specificity is often what allows people to recognise themselves, their relatives or their communities in the work.

<a id="2-the-three-stories-within-the-story"></a>
# **2. The three stories within the story**

A useful publicity brief can hold three interlocking stories at once. The balance will vary between productions, and not every release or interview needs to contain all three.

## **The production story**

This is the story of the creative decision: why this actor, why this interpretation, why disability mattered or why it was allowed to exist without explanation. It can include casting, writing, design, collaboration and the reason the production felt more alive because of the choice.

## **The talent story**

This is the story the actor chooses to tell about their craft, career and connection to the material. Disability may be an important part of that story, but it is not automatically the beginning, middle and end of it.

## **The audience story**

This is the invitation to the audience. It considers what viewers may recognise in the character and relationships: being underestimated, negotiating independence, finding love, communicating differently, or seeing a world that does not erase disability.

A strong piece of publicity usually moves between these layers. It begins with the production and the performance, allows the actor to speak with agency, and opens a door for audiences to find the story in themselves.

<a id="3-permission-ownership-and-boundaries"></a>
# **3. Permission, ownership and boundaries**

A performer's disability story belongs to them. Casting them does not transfer ownership of their medical history, diagnosis, trauma, family experience or route into the industry to the production or marketing team.

## **Treat permission as a creative conversation**

A standard release form confirms legal permission to use material. It does not replace a conversation about meaning, emphasis and boundaries. The most productive approach is to agree the story with the talent and the production team in advance.

The conversation can separate different kinds of permission:

- Permission to identify the actor as disabled, D/deaf, neurodivergent or by another term they choose.
- Permission to discuss the actor's condition, impairment or access requirements, if they wish to make these public.
- Permission to discuss personal history, including education, training, career barriers, discrimination, family or acquired disability.
- Permission to discuss experiences during the production, including adjustments, creative disagreements, research and collaboration.
- Permission to connect the actor's personal experience directly with the character, rather than allowing the two to remain separate.

## **Agree the shape of disclosure**

Consent is easier to maintain when the talent can see how their story may be used. A short story map can identify the proposed angle, the level of detail, the intended outlets and the material that is not up for discussion.

This is particularly important when publicity moves between formats. An actor may be comfortable discussing a subject in a long-form interview but not in a headline, short social clip, red-carpet camera or other high-visibility context.

## **Allow a meaningful right of review**

Where personal disability experience is part of the copy, the actor or an agreed representative can review the relevant passages, pull quotes, captions and headline framing. The purpose is not to censor or police the actor’s story; it is to protect consent and keep the story true to their own boundaries.

## **Respect the decision not to tell**

There can still be a powerful publicity story when an actor does not wish to disclose the origin of a disability or a private experience. The focus can remain on performance, character, authorship and the creative process.

## **Do not turn talent into unpaid consultancy**

An actor may choose to contribute ideas about portrayal, scripts, access, language or publicity. When this becomes work beyond their acting and ordinary promotional responsibilities, the production should treat it as professional labour, with appropriate agreement, payment and credit where relevant.

<a id="4-starting-the-publicity-brief"></a>
# **4. Starting the publicity brief**

The disability story is easiest to find when publicity is involved before the final press release is drafted. A short discussion with casting, production, access, writers, directors and talent can reveal where the story is strongest.

## **Begin with the creative proposition**

A useful opening sentence for the brief is not “This production includes a disabled actor.” It is a statement about what became more interesting, truthful or distinctive because of the casting.

- The casting changed the balance of power in a familiar relationship.
- A character from established source material was brought to screen with lived experience for the first time.
- The actor’s physical knowledge shaped movement, action or intimacy.
- A historical figure’s disability was restored to a life story from which it is often omitted.
- An incidental casting choice made the fictional world feel less uniform and more believable.
- The production chose not to diagnose a coded character, but worked with a performer whose lived experience gave the behaviour consistency and depth.

## **Map the people who hold the story**

The story may sit with more than the lead actor. Publicity can identify contributions from:

- the actor and their agent or publicist;
- the writer or showrunner;
- the director;
- the casting director;
- other actors whose relationships were affected by the interpretation;
- the creative disability consultant, Deaf consultant, movement specialist, intimacy coordinator, dialect or signing specialist;
- production design, costume, prosthetics, stunts, visual effects, sound and editorial teams;
- disabled historians, researchers, community advisers or family representatives;
- the access coordinator, where access methods are part of the story and disclosure has been agreed.

## **Find the turning point**

Most compelling production stories contain a moment when the work changed. Publicity can look for the point at which the creative team stopped treating disability as a problem to solve and began understanding it as an artistic source of depth, complexity or possibility.

<a id="5-putting-talent-and-craft-first"></a>
# **5. Putting talent and craft first**

The first description of an actor can usually establish their role, performance and creative achievement before discussing disability. This signals that they are present because of their talent, not simply because they are a representation point.

## **Lead with the work**

A talent-led profile can begin with what the actor does on screen: the control of a scene, the emotional choices, comic timing, physical precision, chemistry, use of silence, authority or vulnerability.

This is different from hiding disability. It places disability within a complete account of the actor rather than using it as the whole account.

## **Move from biography to artistic knowledge**

Barriers faced entering the industry can be important, especially when the actor chooses to discuss inaccessible training, assumptions about the roles they could play, or the lack of auditions. The story can still remain about craft as well as identity.

## **Avoid the “overcoming” shortcut**

Publicity often reaches for a simple arc in which a performer overcame their condition to act. This may erase the real barriers, including inaccessible buildings, narrow casting assumptions, inflated expectations, or a lack of support.

## **Do not ask one actor to represent everyone**

Every disability has a personal story, but no single actor embodies the full experience of a condition or community. Publicity can be precise: this is how this performer describes their life, this is how they approached the work, and that is enough.

<a id="6-exploring-character-relationships-and-emotion"></a>
# **6. Exploring character, relationships and emotion**

The disability story of a production is not located only in the disabled character. It also exists in the behaviour of everyone around them. Relationships reveal whether the production understands disability as a relational and social issue, not just a trait or medical detail.

## **Look beyond the character description**

A release or feature can explore how disability changes the emotional temperature between characters. It may affect who makes decisions, who is believed, who offers help, how help is refused, how intimacy is negotiated, or how power is distributed.

## **Relationships can carry the most recognisable story**

The publicity angle may sit in:

- siblings negotiating protection, trust and independence;
- friends learning when to assist and when to step back;
- lovers working through exposure, desire, bodily confidence and interdependence;
- parents confronting their own fear, bias or expectations;
- colleagues responding to authority, competence and difference;
- rivals using prejudice, underestimation or access barriers as part of the conflict;
- communities developing shared language, routines or technologies;
- a disabled character misunderstanding or being misunderstood by others, without the production making either side simplistic.

## **Explore the other performers' work**

Other actors can often describe how the casting changed their own performance. They may have had to communicate differently, reconsider physical proximity, learn sign language, change timing, question their assumptions or rethink their emotional behaviour.

## **Make room for love, humour and contradiction**

Disability publicity can become earnest even when the production is not. Features can include flirtation, comic failure, anger, ambition, selfishness, eroticism, boredom, danger and joy. A character is not defined by one issue alone.

<a id="7-telling-the-creative-journey"></a>
# **7. Telling the creative journey**

Audiences are interested in how performances are made. Disability can provide unusually rich behind-the-scenes material because it often asks departments to think more carefully about movement, sound, communication, access, design and performance detail.

## **The writer's journey**

Publicity can explore what the writer first understood, what they researched, what changed after meeting disabled creatives, and where they deliberately resisted explanation. Useful material may include process notes, conversations with the writer, or a reflection on earlier drafts.

## **The director's journey**

The director can describe how the actor altered blocking, visual language, pace, point of view or emotional emphasis. The most interesting story is rarely that the director made accommodations. It is that the work became more precise, more integrated and more alive because of an informed creative choice.

## **The actor's journey**

The actor may discuss research, physical or vocal choices, personal connections, choices to reveal or withhold parts of their own experience, and moments when the character felt different from the person.

## **The production's methods**

Possible areas for features, video and photography include:

- how a mobility aid, prosthesis, cane, hearing technology or communication device was integrated into performance and design;
- how action, dance, combat or movement was built around the actor's strengths rather than copied from a non-disabled template;
- how sound, silence, signing, captions or point-of-view sequences were used to tell the story;
- how costume, sets and locations supported both the character and the performer;
- how access coordination, scheduling or communication created the conditions for better creative work;
- how the edit protected the actor's performance rather than using disability as visual shorthand;
- how the actor's ideas affected another department or another character.

## **Do not confuse access with the creative story**

Access can be part of publicity when the talent wishes it to be and when it demonstrates a useful production method. It should not be presented as generosity or as evidence that the actor was different in a way that needs to be praised or excused.

<a id="8-source-material-established-universes-and-literature"></a>
# **8. Source material, established universes and literature**

Adaptations and established universes offer a particularly strong publicity opportunity because audiences already care about the character, the canon and previous interpretations. Marvel, DC, Star Wars, Shakespeare, classics, historical narrative and literary adaptations all create possibilities for a richer disability story.

## **When disability is part of the original text**

The publicity story can examine the route from page, comic panel, game or earlier screen version to the new performance. Areas to explore include:

- which disability characteristics were explicit in the source;
- which were implied through behaviour, language, movement, technology or social treatment;
- whether previous adaptations reduced, exaggerated or erased them;
- how the casting search responded to the source;
- what the actor recognised in the character and what they challenged;
- how costume, prosthetics, effects, sound, choreography and production design translated lived experience;
- what was retained, reinterpreted or transposed, and the creative reason for that decision;
- how the adaptation may change what existing fans understand about the character.

## **When the actor's disability changes the screen version**

Sometimes the screen character develops around the actor. Publicity can explain this as adaptation rather than deviation: the production selected a performer, understood what their body, language, movement and creative choice could contribute, and built the character around that understanding.

## **When disability is introduced into an adaptation**

A character may not have been disabled in the original version. The publicity opportunity is to explain what the choice unlocked: a new relationship, a different survival strategy, a communication style, a change in vulnerability, or a different mode of power.

## **Be careful with “firsts” and fidelity claims**

Established universes have long histories across formats and countries. Claims such as “the first disabled character” or “the first authentic portrayal” are easy to get wrong and can oversimplify the history of the story, the character and the audience response.

<a id="9-disabled-historical-figures"></a>
# **9. Disabled historical figures**

When a production portrays a disabled historical figure, publicity can restore disability to a life from which biographies and previous dramas may have removed it. The aim is not to turn the person into a disability symbol; it is to acknowledge the actual lived experience and the material conditions of the period.

## **Start with the whole life**

The figure's achievements, contradictions, relationships and historical significance remain central. Disability is explored as part of that life rather than as its explanation. This avoids both erasure and reduction.

## **Show the research and the limits of certainty**

Historical evidence may include letters, diaries, portraits, medical records, equipment, contemporary descriptions, architecture and accounts from other people. Modern diagnoses can be uncertain and often require careful research.

## **Explore the world around the person**

The story can include the technologies, physical environments, language, social status and attitudes of the period. The way a person moved, communicated or was treated by society is part of the story.

## **Respect families, communities and contested histories**

Where appropriate, the production may engage descendants, estates, historians or communities. Their involvement can be acknowledged without implying that one person or organisation owns the interpretation.

<a id="10-explicit-coded-integrated-and-incidental-portrayal"></a>
# **10. Explicit, coded, integrated and incidental portrayal**

Different forms of portrayal create different publicity stories. Naming the type internally can help a team find the right emphasis, although the labels do not always need to appear in public copy.

## **Explicit disability**

The character is clearly disabled within the text or on screen. The story may name the disability, show access needs, or place it within the plot. Publicity can explore authentic casting, lived experience, and the production's creative reasoning around representation.

## **Coded disability**

The character has behaviours, physical characteristics, communication patterns, technologies, social experiences or language that audiences may read as disability, but the production does not present them in a medicalised or overtly diagnostic way.

## **Integrated portrayal**

Disability is recognised when it affects the situation, but it is not discussed in every scene. This often resembles real life: access, fatigue, communication or prejudice matter at some moments and are not the centre of every conversation.

## **Incidental disability**

The actor is disabled but the role was not written around disability and the production does not explain it. Publicity can tell the story of creative openness: why the actor was right, what their presence changed, and how disability became part of the world without needing to be explained.

## **Why the distinction matters**

The four approaches create a portfolio of representation. Explicit stories can examine disability directly. Coded stories can preserve ambiguity and invite interpretation. Integrated stories can reflect real-world complexity. Incidental stories can normalise disability as part of the lived environment. Each has a different publicity value.

<a id="11-aligning-publicity-with-organisational-commitments"></a>
# **11. Aligning publicity with organisational commitments**

Studios, broadcasters and funders increasingly commit to authentic portrayal, lived-experience casting, open and fair auditions, incidental representation, the Social Model of Disability, accessibility and cultural accountability.

## **Use the commitment as a route into evidence**

A policy statement becomes credible when the press materials can show what happened in practice. The following connections can help:

- Authentic portrayal: explain whose lived experience informed the character and how their ideas affected the work.
- Open and fair casting: tell the discovery story, the search process or the audition that changed the team’s understanding of the role.
- Disabled actors in roles not written as disabled: show why the performer was the most interesting choice and what their casting added.
- Nothing about us without us: identify the disabled people with creative agency, not merely those consulted after decisions were made.
- Removing barriers: describe the production method that enabled talent to contribute fully, without framing access as benevolence.
- Social Model thinking: focus on relationships, environments and attitudes rather than presenting the disabled body as the problem.
- Nuanced, three-dimensional portrayal: discuss ambition, sexuality, humour, moral complexity, authority and contradiction.
- Measurement and accountability: use figures to provide context, but do not make a target the emotional centre of the talent story.

## **Avoid the commitment-as-congratulation problem**

A production can meet an organisational commitment without asking the audience to applaud the organisation for employing a disabled person. Publicity is stronger when the institution's role is described in context rather than as the point of the story.

## **Connect brand values with creative identity**

Where an organisation speaks about belonging, equity, authenticity or reflecting audiences, the publicity can demonstrate how those values influenced a specific choice. This moves inclusion from a statement into an observed creative result.

<a id="12-formats-assets-and-accessible-publicity"></a>
# **12. Formats, assets and accessible publicity**

The disability story can be distributed across a campaign rather than compressed into one press release. Different formats allow different people to speak and reduce the pressure on the disabled talent to answer all questions in one place.

## **Potential formats**

- A long-form interview centred on the actor’s craft and chosen personal connections.
- A joint interview between the disabled actor and the performer playing a key relationship.
- A writer-director feature on adaptation, research and the decisions that changed during development.
- A casting story about how the role was opened, reinterpreted or shaped around the actor.
- A production craft feature on movement, stunts, sound, design, costume, prosthetics, signing or visual language.
- A short behind-the-scenes film in which talent and crew each describe one creative discovery.
- A source-material feature for fans, comparing the character’s history with the new interpretation.
- A historical research feature distinguishing evidence, interpretation and invention.
- A relationship-led feature about love, friendship, family, rivalry, power or interdependence.
- A first-person piece commissioned from the talent, with editorial control and appropriate payment.

## **Build an asset bank that does not medicalise**

Photography and video can show the actor working, rehearsing, collaborating and inhabiting the character. Access equipment or disability characteristics do not need to be hidden, but they also do not need to be turned into a display object.

Captions and image descriptions can name the actor, character, action, expression and setting before describing disability-related detail when it is visually relevant. This prevents the disabled identity from being the only thing the audience notices.

## **Make the publicity itself accessible**

A campaign cannot credibly celebrate disabled audiences while excluding them from the material. Useful provisions include accurate captions, transcripts, audio description or descriptive introductions, BSL interpretation, and accessible event planning.

Accessibility can also create better assets. A signed interview may reveal a performer's expressiveness more fully. Audio description can become a creative introduction. A transcript can extend the life and reach of an interview.

<a id="13-interview-questions-that-open-up-better-stories"></a>
# **13. Interview questions that open up better stories**

The following prompts are not a fixed questionnaire. They are designed to help publicity teams move away from diagnosis and toward performance, authorship, relationships and production craft.

## **Questions for the actor**

- What first interested you in the character beyond the way they were described on the page?
- Which parts of your own experience did you choose to draw on, and which parts did you deliberately keep separate?
- What did you understand about this character that the creative team had not yet considered?
- Was there a scene in which your interpretation changed the relationship between the characters?
- What physical, vocal or emotional choices were most important to building the performance?
- How did the production methods support you in doing your best work?
- What assumptions about you or the role did the performance allow you to challenge?
- Which audiences or relationships do you hope will recognise something of themselves in the character?
- Is there a part of your personal story that you are comfortable connecting with the production, and how would you like that connection to be described?
- What would you prefer the publicity not to make central?

## **Questions for writers and directors**

- What changed after you began working with the actor?
- How did the casting alter the way you understood power, vulnerability, humour or intimacy in the story?
- Which parts of the portrayal came from research, which came from the actor, and which emerged in rehearsal?
- How did you decide when disability needed to be acknowledged and when it could remain incidental?
- What did you learn about the other characters through their responses to disability?
- Was there a moment when a practical access decision became a creative advantage?
- What did you choose not to explain to the audience, and why?
- How did organisational commitments influence the process without dictating the art?

## **Questions for other actors**

- How did this casting affect the way you played your relationship with the character?
- Did you have to reconsider how your character offers help, exerts control, communicates or shows affection?
- What did you learn from the other performer's process?
- Which scene best captures the trust, conflict or understanding between the characters?

## **Questions for production departments**

- What did you design around the performer rather than asking the performer to fit?
- Which creative problem produced the most original solution?
- How did sound, movement, costume, sets, props, effects or editing express the character's point of view?
- What detail might audiences not notice consciously but will feel in the finished work?

## **Questions to use with care or replace**

Questions such as “What happened to you?”, “How did you overcome your disability?”, “What can you not do?” or “How does it feel to inspire people?” narrow the story before the actual creative content has been explored.

<a id="14-language-and-framing"></a>
# **14. Language and framing**

Language preferences differ between individuals, communities and countries. The talent's chosen terms are the starting point. Publicity teams can record them in the brief so that press releases, interviews and copy remain consistent.

## **Describe identity without making it a verdict**

Identity-first language such as “disabled actor”, “Deaf actor” or “autistic actor” is widely used and may express culture and identity. Some people prefer person-first language such as “actor with a disability”. The preference should be respected.

## **Prefer barriers and experience to deficit**

Words such as “suffers from”, “confined to a wheelchair”, “victim of”, “normal” and “able-bodied” often introduce pity, inaccuracy or comparison. More neutral wording can describe a person, a situation and a lived experience without reducing them to a medical or moral category.

## **Use achievement language carefully**

“Inspirational”, “brave” and “extraordinary” can be appropriate when they describe the work, the risk of a performance or the actor's own language. They become reductive when ordinary achievement is being discussed without context.

## **Use “cripping up” when it adds clarity**

The term “cripping up” describes casting a non-disabled or non-visibly-disabled actor to mimic an apparent disability in a disabled role or historical figure. It is useful in industry discussion and can be included when it helps explain a problem or decision.

## **Avoid the checkbox story**

Phrases such as “ticking a box”, even when used defensively, can reinforce the idea that the performer represents an administrative requirement. The release can instead describe the creative decision and the artistry involved.

<a id="15-examples-of-strong-publicity-angles"></a>
# **15. Examples of strong publicity angles**

These examples illustrate different ways a campaign can tell a disability story while retaining talent, craft and consent at the centre.

## **All the Light We Cannot See: the casting process as creative conviction**

Netflix publicity did more than state that blind actors Aria Mia Loberti and Nell Sutton played Marie-Laure. It showed auditions, described the search, and allowed director Shawn Levy to explain how the casting transformed the story.

## **Echo: an actor reshaping a superhero**

Publicity around Echo presented Alaqua Cox as the lead and action performer, while also allowing her to discuss being Deaf, Indigenous and an amputee on her own terms. Behind-the-scenes material focused on performance, action and story rather than victimhood.

## **We Might Regret This: a boundary as an expression of authorship**

Kyla Harris has discussed disability, intimacy, care, shame, autonomy and the creative origins of the series while declining to disclose how she became disabled. Publicity could still explore deep creative questions without demanding personal disclosure.

## **Better: lived experience as performance method**

Zak Ford-Williams’ role in Better created a behind-the-scenes story about performance technique. He initially masked his cerebral palsy and then revealed disability as the character experienced it, making the performance more truthful.

## **Incidental casting: the role that did not need an explanation**

Where disability is incidental, a feature may be built around why the actor was right for the part and how their presence changed the ensemble. The absence of an origin story can itself be part of the artistic choice.

## **Historical portrayal: restoring a missing dimension**

For a disabled historical figure, the angle may be that disability has often been omitted from public memory even though it affected the person’s daily life, relationships and work. The feature can also examine the production’s research methods and historical context.

<a id="16-publicity-brief-template"></a>
# **16. Publicity brief template**

The following structure can be adapted for a campaign document, press pack planning session or talent briefing.

## **A. Creative proposition**

In one or two sentences, describe what disability, lived experience or the casting decision made more interesting about the production.

## **B. Talent and story ownership**

- Who wishes to speak about the disability story?
- Which identity terms do they use?
- Which personal experiences can be discussed?
- Which subjects, images or details are private?
- Who approves personal copy, quotes and captions?
- Does any advisory contribution require separate payment or credit?

## **C. Character and relationship story**

- What does the character want beyond disability?
- Which relationships reveal barriers, bias, love, power, humour or understanding?
- How did the casting change another character or performance?
- What emotional connection may disabled audiences and their loved ones recognise?

## **D. Creative journey**

- What changed during writing, casting, rehearsal or filming?
- What came from the actor’s ideas or lived experience?
- What production method is worth showing?
- Who else should be credited for the work?

## **E. Source and context**

- Is disability explicit, coded, integrated or incidental?
- Is the character drawn from literature, comics, games, an established universe or history?
- What was documented in the source?
- What was retained, transposed, introduced or left ambiguous?
- Are claims about history, diagnosis or “firsts” fully verified?

## **F. Organisational alignment**

- Which organisational commitment is relevant?
- What practical evidence demonstrates that commitment?
- Can the value be shown through creative outcomes rather than corporate self-congratulation?

## **G. Formats and assets**

- Which story belongs in the press release, and which needs a longer format?
- What interviews, stills, rehearsal material, design images or behind-the-scenes footage are available?
- Are captions, transcripts, audio description, image descriptions and signed or interpreted material planned?
- Are press events and interview arrangements accessible?

## **H. Audience invitation**

End the brief with the human connection. What does the production invite audiences to feel, recognise, reconsider or discuss?

<a id="17-opportunity-review-before-publication"></a>
# **17. Opportunity review before publication**

This short review is intended to improve the story rather than police it. A publicity team can ask:

- Does the piece lead with talent, character or creative purpose rather than diagnosis?
- Has the actor given informed permission for every personal detail and context of use?
- Does the copy preserve the distinction between the performer and the character?
- Are barriers described as features of industries, attitudes or environments where that is the more accurate account?
- Does the story include relationships, emotions and other characters rather than isolating disability within one person?
- Is the writer, director and production journey specific enough to show how the work was made?
- Are organisational commitments supported by evidence from the production?
- Are source-material and historical claims accurate and appropriately qualified?
- Does the piece avoid pity, medical voyeurism, heroism for ordinary activity and checkbox language?
- Has everyone who contributed expertise been credited and, where appropriate, paid?
- Can disabled audiences access the publicity material and the event around it?
- Is the final story interesting even after the representation claim is removed?

<a id="18-closing-perspective"></a>
# **18. Closing perspective**

Disability gives publicity departments more to work with, not less. It can reveal a casting decision that transformed a role, an actor whose route into the industry shaped a distinctive practice, a relationship that changed because a character was written or played differently, or a kind of creative courage that makes the work feel more alive.

The opportunity is not to extract a personal story from every disabled performer. It is to create the conditions in which talent can decide what to share, and then to connect that chosen experience to the artistry of the production without reducing either one to a slogan.
