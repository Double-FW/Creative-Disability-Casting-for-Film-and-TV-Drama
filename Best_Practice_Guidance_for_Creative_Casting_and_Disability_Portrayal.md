# Best Practice Guidance for Creative Casting and Disability Portrayal in Scripted Television and Film

## Purpose and central argument

The principal purpose of storytelling and characterisation is to help audiences learn about one another and broaden their understanding of what it is to be human. That purpose is weakened when the characters on screen do not reflect the breadth of society.

Disabled people remain underrepresented in many forms of scripted television and film, particularly in roles where disability is incidental rather than the subject of the story. Reliable UK sources currently estimate that disabled people make up approximately one quarter of the population, but no equally robust population measure exists for “visible” or “apparent” disability. The source article's claim that visibly disabled people make up at least 10% of society should therefore be treated as an advocacy estimate, not an established demographic fact.

Disability-related stories have received significant awards attention. The linked [IMDb collection of Academy Award examples](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls597302276/?ref_=uspf_ttl_1) illustrates this pattern, but it is a curated list rather than an authoritative statistical analysis. Awards success demonstrates industry interest; it does not, by itself, establish audience demand or the quality and authenticity of a portrayal.

The stronger creative case is that disability expands the range of bodies, communication styles, relationships, histories and forms of agency available to storytelling. Working with disabled talent can create richer characterisation while opening professional opportunities that have historically been restricted.

## Scope

This guide is intended for production companies and creative teams working on scripted television and film. It can inform development, writing, commissioning, casting, rehearsal, production, post-production and publicity.

It draws on production examples and industry guidance, including [TripleC's guidance for inclusive writers' rooms](https://triplec.org.uk/writers-room-guidlines/). It addresses:

- casting without disability bias;
- authentic and incidental portrayal;
- finding disabled performers;
- creative disability consultancy and access coordination;
- identifying disability characteristics in scripts;
- historical disability and evidence quality;
- creating castable disabled characters;
- common portrayal and production failures;
- action, movement and stunt work; and
- governance, privacy, consent and human review.

## Core definitions

| Term | Working definition for this guide |
|---|---|
| **Disabled person** | A person who experiences disability through the interaction of an impairment, health condition or neurotype with attitudinal, environmental, organisational or communication barriers. Identity and preferred language vary. |
| **Authentic casting** | Casting a disabled performer whose lived experience has a meaningful relationship to the disability embodied, communicated or narratively established in the role. It does not always require an identical diagnosis. |
| **Incidental portrayal** | A disabled performer playing a character whose disability is present but is not the character's principal narrative purpose. |
| **Condition transposition** | Adapting a character's impairment or disability characteristics to align more closely with the performer while preserving the relevant narrative function and lived experience. |
| **Cripping-up** | A critical term for casting a non-disabled performer as a disabled character, particularly where disability is embodied or central to the role. Use the term with awareness that it is contested and may require explanation. |
| **Creative disability consultant** | A creative adviser supporting story, character, casting and portrayal decisions through disability-related knowledge and relevant creative expertise. |
| **Access coordinator** | A specialist who helps identify and coordinate practical access requirements for disabled cast and crew. This is distinct from creative portrayal consultancy. |
| **Access rider** | A performer- or worker-led description of adjustments, working conditions or support that enables equitable participation. It is not proof of diagnosis. |
| **Apparent disability** | Disability characteristics that an audience may reasonably perceive through embodiment, movement, communication, assistive technology, dialogue, subtext or narrative information. Appearance alone must not be used to diagnose a person. |

## Casting without disability bias

> “Creativity is about making interesting choices, and disability is always an interesting choice.”
>
> — Zak Ford-Williams, actor

### *How does a character enter the room if this action is not described in the script?*

This question is used by disability consultants to expose assumptions embedded in casting. Why presume that an unspecified character walks? Is there a story-based, practical or safety-critical reason that the character must be non-disabled?

If a specific condition, impairment or characteristic cannot be transferred to a character, then it is also reasonable to assume that other disability characteristics can.

This opens up a huge number of casting possibilities as disabled people in real life are husbands, wives, friends, lovers, brothers, sisters, teachers, engineers, politicians, bureaucrats, criminals, scientists… They are people.

People can be good, bad or anything in-between. Don’t worry about the nature of a character. People can be strong, centred, vulnerable or deeply flawed, whether or not they have a disability.

The source article estimates that more than 90% of scripted characters could reasonably be played by a disabled person. This is a creative challenge, not a measured industry statistic. Its useful meaning is that disability should not be treated as disqualifying unless a role has a specific and defensible requirement.

An open approach has some similarities to what has been called “[colour-blind casting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color-blind_casting)”, although “colour-conscious casting” is often the more useful comparison because identity, power and context should not be ignored. Disability-conscious casting can expand opportunity while recognising that embodiment, access, communication and audience meaning remain part of the creative decision.

**Casting disabled actors as a process has two creative lenses:**

1. **Disability and incidental portrayal.**

Despite most characters in a production being able to be played by disabled actors, disabled actors are rarely given the opportunity to portray them. A method used at the start of the process is to look at each character in isolation and ask the question, ***“why can’t a disabled actor be cast in this role?”,*** with the follow-up, ***“what would disability bring to this role?”***

These questions give the creatives an opportunity to consider how disability could add further dimensions to a role not otherwise considered.

A good example is the character Sam Burrell played by [Keivonn Woodard](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm13546659/?ref_=ext_shr_lnk) in HBO’s [The Last of Us](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt3581920/?ref_=ext_shr_lnk). In the video game neither of the boys were Deaf, but in the TV series there was a decision made to write disability in the role as this added subtext and a new dynamic in their relationship with the protagonists.

2. **Disability and authentic portrayal.**

Where disability is embodied, communicated or central to the narrative, cast as authentically as possible. A signing role requires a performer with the appropriate language competence; a wheelchair-user role should ordinarily go to an appropriate wheelchair user; and neurodivergent characters should be developed and cast with relevant lived experience in mind.

If the character is a known [disabled literary figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_characters_with_disabilities) or historical person, aim for authenticity without reducing the role to diagnostic matching. The lived experience, social meaning and functional characteristics relevant to the story may matter more than an identical clinical label. A creative disability consultant can help the team distinguish legitimate transposition from erasure or inauthentic embodiment.

## Casting Resources for Finding Disabled Actors

More disabled actors are entering the profession through drama schools, comedy, [disability-led theatre companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability#Contemporary_disability-led_theatre_companies) and other performing arts. The source article described the professional pool as “only a few hundred”, but no authoritative current count was identified. Treat the available pool as under-recorded and unevenly represented by agents and databases, not inherently too small.

When exploring casting possibilities, distinguish characteristics essential to the story from details that can be adapted. Excessively narrow breakdowns can make a role unnecessarily difficult to cast, but widening the brief must not erase language, culture, embodiment or lived experience that the story actually depends upon.

**Here are some lists of disabled and neurodivergent stage and screen actors with either links to their work or showreels to help with casting research:**

- [ProFile, a video database of D/deaf and disabled actors](https://profileperformers.com/)

- [IMDb List of Disabled and Deaf Actors](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls541464229/?ref_=uspf_ttl_3)

- [IMDb List of Neurodivergent Actors](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls545872590/?ref_=uspf_ttl_10)

- [Wikipedia, Actors with Disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Actors_with_disabilities)

- [Disabled Voice Actors](https://www.disabledvoiceactors.com/)

Offer every candidate a clear, confidential route for requesting adjustments. Involve an access coordinator where the scale, complexity or duration of the production makes that useful; do not make access coordination a condition of auditioning. A performer may use an [access rider](https://www.littlecog.co.uk/artist-access-riders.html), but must not be presumed to have one. Ask the performer or their authorised representative what is needed, share only operationally necessary information, and do not circulate diagnosis or medical history as casting material.

## What is a Creative Disability Consultant?

This role is very similar to a [dramaturge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturge). It can be utilised by any TV or film production that is creating, casting or telling a story about disability. They can also be brought in to help explore the possibility of casting disabled actors in roles not scripted as disabled.

This is not to be confused with an [Access Co-ordinator](https://triplec.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Access-Coordinators-in-Film-and-TV-Productions-24.08.22.pdf) which is a completely different type of specialist who focuses on the practicalities of working with disabled talent.

Engage a creative disability consultant early enough to influence decisions: ideally during development or before the casting brief is fixed. Later involvement can still help, but must not be used merely to validate choices already made.

The engagement progresses beyond casting, working with the Director, actors and writers, focusing on aspects of storytelling and meaning. The aim is to ensure that both authenticity and creative opportunity stay forefront.

The consultant's authority should be defined in the brief. They may advise, research, challenge, workshop or co-create, depending on their contract and expertise. They are not automatically the final decision-maker, but neither should “consultation” mean that their advice can be solicited, ignored and then cited as approval. No individual consultant can represent every disability community.

### The Creative Disability Consultant

- **Should receive a written brief covering development, casting, script advice, rehearsal, production, edit and publicity where appropriate.**

- **Should have a clear advisory or decision-making remit, with an agreed escalation route when advice is not followed.**

- **Should have relevant creative and disability-related expertise. Personal lived experience is valuable but is not, by itself, competence across every condition or community.**

- **Should be appropriately contracted, remunerated and credited.**

Do not treat a disabled performer's presence as free consultancy. If a performer is asked to provide script, portrayal, language, cultural or access advice beyond ordinary performance work, agree the scope, authority, credit and additional remuneration with them and their representative in advance.

## The Brief for Hiring a Creative Disability Consultant

Having a creative disability consultant can enable greater creative confidence from the writer’s room to the final edit. They provide an extra sense of experience and perspective, supporting the creative team in their decision making.

1. **Research.** They provide research, especially if the script features disabled literary or historical figures. They focus on the history, sociology, art, culture and linguistics of the work. This includes providing practical advice on the difference between literal condition portrayal, and the portrayal of the lived experience of disability. This can also include ideas like transposing a character’s visible condition to that of the actor if the attitudes and experience are an important factor rather than literalism. They also help to explore the impact of introducing an apparent (visible) disability not just on the character in question but the relationships of other characters with them.

2. **Context.** They should share insight into lived experience with production teams including the Directors, Writers, Show Runners and Actors, to ensure common understanding.

3. **Script Advice.** Disability can be a story, but it can also add new dimensions to a character or plot. It is not just an aspect of a character but the attitudes of those around them that lend themselves to the authenticity of portrayal. Having a creative advisor will enable writers to feel more comfortable exploring possibilities, and provide a safe and creative space for exploration and feedback.

4. **Casting Advice.** When casting disabled actors there are several things a consultant can help with. When it comes to progressive conditions, the consultant can work with the Director on the practicalities of masking and revealing a condition to the audience. They also work with the Casting Director to identify roles suitable for incidental portrayal.

5. **Production Advisor.** If the production is more fluid and the script evolves as part of the filming process, then having a consultant on-hand to work with the creative team to help evaluate their ideas will give everyone greater confidence, opening up more creative possibility without losing authenticity.

## Identifying Disability in a Script

When identifying disability in a script, look for functional, embodied, social and narrative characteristics rather than waiting for a diagnostic label. Older source material may describe disability through action, language, relationships or technology without using modern identity terms.

Cast as closely as the material requires, while recognising that relevant lived experience may matter more than exact diagnostic matching. Some transpositions are unlikely to work: a signing character needs an appropriately fluent signer; an embodied wheelchair-user role ordinarily needs a suitable wheelchair user; and one form of lived experience should not be treated as interchangeable with an unrelated one merely because both fall under the word “disability”.

Condition transposition can work when the production preserves the relevant embodiment, barriers, social meaning and character relationships. Examples for discussion include [Peter Dinklage as Cyrano](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt12889404/?ref_=ls_mv_desc) and the [different disabled actors who have played Richard III](https://www.reddit.com/r/disability/comments/1h2iphs/disabled_actors_playing_richard_iii/). The Reddit discussion is community commentary, not authoritative evidence.

Identifying characteristics is interpretive work, especially with literary and historical texts. Modern disability identity and terminology cannot simply be projected backwards, but disability itself is not a modern invention. The linked claim that the [Shakespearean canon contains about 25 disabled characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up#On_Stage) is a critical interpretation whose exact count depends on the definition used.

**Apparent disability characteristics can include:**

- **Assistive technology and equipment:** wheelchairs, walking sticks, crutches, white canes, hearing aids, cochlear implants, assistance animals, prostheses, speech-generating devices, ostomy equipment, mobility scooters, splints and ear defenders.

- **Speech and vocal expression:** stammering or stuttering, being nonspeaking, vocal tics, dysarthria or other speech differences. Do not assume speech difference implies cognitive difference.

- **Communication and language:** use of a sign language, lip-reading, augmentative and alternative communication, communication partners or differences in reading and written-language access. Do not equate literacy with intelligence.

- **Speculative or futuristic technology:** robotic limbs, movement aids, sensory technologies and life-support systems. Consider who controls the technology and whether it represents access, surveillance, dependence, power or identity.

- **Health-related characteristics:** seizures, continence requirements, immune vulnerability, fatigue, pain, fluctuating capacity or sensitivity to the environment. Avoid using distress, overload or involuntary behaviour merely as spectacle.

- **Mobility:** use of mobility aids, assisted walking, pain, fatigue, variable mobility or ambulatory wheelchair use.

- **Movement:** spatial awareness, tics, stimming, spasms, tremors, gait differences, paralysis, balance, coordination, pain, fatigue, facial movement differences, physical injury and fine- or gross-motor control.

- **Physical characteristics:** limb differences, surgical scars, short stature, albinism, skeletal or muscular differences and characteristics associated with Down syndrome.

- **Sensory characteristics:** Blindness, partial sight, Deafness, hearing differences and Deafblindness. Identity and preferred terminology vary.

Think about what the experience of the character would be in terms of prejudice, social isolation, attitudes, ableism, culture and experience of access to the physical world.

## Disability and Historical Accuracy

Disabled people have existed in every historical period and social class. Their lives were shaped by wealth, gender, race, status, work, family, institutions, warfare, disease, access to support and the attitudes of their time. Some became scholars, teachers, religious figures, courtiers, advisers, craftspeople, performers or rulers; others were excluded, institutionalised or left economically vulnerable.

Court fools were a diverse group. Some historical records describe people with physical, cognitive or learning disabilities, while others were professional entertainers without known impairments. Do not presume that every fool or jester was disabled, but do include disability in the historical and casting research.

War, infectious disease, dangerous labour, malnutrition and limited healthcare made injury and impairment common. This supports a wider range of bodies in historical casting, but does not justify assigning an invented diagnosis to every character.

The merchant classes had the same issues with healthcare and workers also had little in the way of protection so physical injury was more commonplace.

Amongst the ruling classes impairments occurred because of disease, poor diets (too much unhealthy food or drink), [STI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_transmitted_infection)s, inbreeding, hunting accidents, duelling or other sports, and poor healthcare with doctors not being able to fix everyday ailments, or prescribing treatments that did more harm than good.

Soldiers returning from war, where many characteristics could be attributed to battlefield injury or [PTSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder).

Religious institutions sometimes offered disabled people education, work, care or status, particularly when family wealth or patronage was available. They could also impose segregation or control. “Safe place” is therefore too simple a description.

There are many pirates, buccaneers, explorers and other seamen in literature both historical such as Sir Walter Raleigh or Edward Teach, or fictional like Long John Silver or Captain Hook.

Life at sea could involve scurvy, occupational injury, violence and punishment. Fictional pirates such as [Pew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Island#Minor) draw on this association, although a fictional convention is not evidence of historical prevalence.

Historical accuracy should include disability, but the claim that mild or moderate conditions were “never identified” is too absolute. Records vary greatly by period, class, institution and condition. Absence of modern diagnostic language may reflect different classification, missing evidence or an author's narrative priorities—not absence of disability.

Some historical figures are important to disability history and should be considered for authentic casting. Disability was documented in many ways even where modern identity language was absent; the History of Parliament discusses [disability at court in early modern England](https://historyofparliament.com/2021/11/25/disability-at-court-in-early-modern-england/). The examples below are prompts for research, not ready-made medical briefs.

### Evidence rule for historical portrayal

Separate three things:

1. **Documented fact:** contemporary records and strong scholarship support the characteristic.
2. **Reasoned interpretation:** historians infer an explanation from available evidence, with uncertainty retained.
3. **Retrospective diagnosis:** a modern condition is assigned after death. Treat this as a hypothesis unless there is unusually strong evidence and scholarly consensus.

Use the documented functional and social experience when the diagnosis is disputed. Consult historians, disabled creatives and relevant cultural or linguistic specialists before fixing the portrayal.

### Historical figures for production research

- [**Alexander Pope**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Pope), one of the most celebrated and quoted poets of all time. He had Pott’s disease (tuberculosis of the spine) in childhood, which stunted his growth and caused a curvature of the spine.

- [**Charles I of England**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England), was unable to walk unaided until his seventh year, having inherited his father’s, “weak legs and ankles”. He has what can be considered as splints: a pair of reinforced boots was made for Charles by the London bone-setter, Edward Stuteville. He also had ‘macroglossia’, where his tongue was too large for his mouth affecting his ability to speak, eat and drink. In 1622 the Venetian ambassador remarked that the prince had, *‘some impediment through the size and length of his tongue which prevents him from expressing himself freely’.*

- [**Emily Dickinson**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Dickinson) experienced significant health problems, but claims that she had epilepsy or a particular facial difference are disputed and should not be treated as established fact without specialist evidence.

- [**Emperor Claudius**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius) was described in ancient sources as having movement and speech differences. Cerebral palsy is one retrospective hypothesis, not a confirmed diagnosis. His portrayal should be grounded in the recorded characteristics and the political use made of them by hostile sources.

- [**Franklin D. Roosevelt**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) had lower-body paralysis and used wheelchairs, braces and assistance. Polio was the contemporary diagnosis, although later researchers have proposed alternatives. His deliberate management of public visibility is central to the portrayal.

- [**Franz Kafka**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka) had tuberculosis, which substantially affected his health and capacity later in life. Accounts of his psychological life require careful sourcing; avoid pathologising language such as “plagued with mental health issues”.

- [**Frida Kahlo**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frida_Kahlo) had polio as a child and sustained extensive injuries in a bus crash at 18, followed by chronic pain, repeated surgery and a late-life leg amputation. The claim that she was born with spina bifida is contested by newer retrospective research and must not be presented as settled fact.

- [**Florence Nightingale**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale) became disabled after returning home from the Crimea after contracting a fever. She never fully recovered and was disabled for the rest of her life.

- [**Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec) had a skeletal condition, commonly but retrospectively identified as pycnodysostosis. “Maroteaux–Lamy syndrome” is a different condition and has been removed as an incorrect synonym. Other health claims, including syphilis, should be sourced separately rather than used to explain his disability without evidence.

- [**Henri Matisse**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Matisse) became disabled later in life following surgery, which resulted in him becoming a wheelchair user.

- [**Henry VIII**](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-leicestershire-64252226)[ was disabled after the age of 44 because of a hunting accident](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-leicestershire-64252226) and he used a number of mobility aids such as walking sticks, an early type of wheelchair and a hoist and pulley system for getting upstairs.

- [**Horatio Nelson, 1st Viscount Nelson**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_Nelson,_1st_Viscount_Nelson) had multiple disabilities from war injuries which included having his right arm amputated, blindness in his right eye and a spinal injury.

- [**James I**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) did not learn to walk until he was five, and at the age of eighteen he was still unsteady on his feet, *‘his gait is bad, composed of erratic steps’*. By the time he ascended the English throne he often leaned on the man next to him. James’s legs may have been weakened in infancy by rickets. Alternatively, Frederick Holmes, emeritus professor of medicine at the University of Kansas, has proposed that James suffered from an hereditary neuro-muscular disorder.

- [**John Milton**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton) became Blind following progressive sight loss. The precise cause remains uncertain; glaucoma is one proposal.

- [**King Richard III**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_of_England) had scoliosis and his Shakespearean character had more pronounced disabilities, so transposing his condition is in keeping with the messages in the play.

- [**Lord Byron**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron) had a clubbed foot with associated movement and mobility issues.

- [**Robert Cecil, 1st Earl of Salisbury**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cecil,_1st_Earl_of_Salisbury) had scoliosis much like Richard III.

- [**Samuel Johnson**](https://farmerofthoughts.co.uk/collected_pieces/samuel-johnson/) had visual and hearing impairments following childhood illness. Tourette syndrome is a retrospective interpretation of contemporary descriptions and should be labelled as such.

- [**Sir Walter Scott**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scott) had polio as a child and as a result had mobility issues throughout his life.

## Creating a Disabled Character

The disabled talent pool is growing through formal training, comedy, independent work and [disability-led theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_and_disability#Contemporary_disability-led_theatre_companies). Disability is highly diverse, so a role combining many narrowly specified characteristics may be difficult to cast. This is a reason to distinguish essential story requirements from adaptable details—not a reason to default to a non-disabled performer.

Two approaches can reduce avoidable casting barriers:

1. **Write the relevant experience, not an unnecessary diagnostic checklist.** Identify the barriers, relationships, embodiment, communication and story functions that matter. Adapt remaining details with the selected performer and consultant.

2. **Develop the role with a performer in mind.** Resources such as [ProFile](https://profileperformers.com/) can support research. If a performer's lived experience informs development, engage them formally in the writers' room or workshop process, with consent, influence, credit and payment. Do not require them to disclose private medical information or turn their life into the storyline.

Before issuing the breakdown, record:

- which characteristics are narratively essential and why;
- which characteristics may be transposed;
- the required language or cultural competence;
- how candidates can request adjustments;
- who may access disability- or adjustment-related information;
- how consultation will be paid and credited; and
- who has authority to approve changes after casting.

## Ten production risks and creative opportunities

It’s always advisable to learn from the previous success and mistakes, identify what went right or wrong, what should have been learned and what could be done differently.

This is the only part of the guidance that gives a few negative examples as it is important to learn from the past. This is not to pass judgement on those productions as advice was not available or known to be sought at the time. Some of these and other examples can be found in this IMDb article on [The Creative Opportunity of Disability](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls542797973/?ref_=ext_shr_lnk).

### 1. Invisibility and erasure

Avoid the “*Harry Potter* effect”: constructing a large fictional population in which disabled children appear to be absent even though disability exists elsewhere in the world. UK charity data indicates that approximately 11% of children are disabled, but the source article's 3% estimate for specified apparent disabilities and its character-by-character Hogwarts calculation are not supported by a cited dataset.

The diagnostic question remains useful without speculative arithmetic: if disabled adults exist in a fictional world, where are its disabled children, and what unintended message is created when they are missing?

This is not necessarily done with intent, but forgetting disabled children exist is not a great message.

### 2. Cripping-up

[Cripping-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripping-up) is a critical term for giving disabled or neurodivergent roles to non-disabled or neurotypical performers, particularly when the disability is embodied or central to the story.

The linked IMDb collections identify [many alleged examples](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls541869888/?ref_=uspf_ttl_2) and claim that [22 of 25 Academy Award winners playing disabled leads were non-disabled](https://www.imdb.com/list/ls543984118/?ref_=uspf_ttl_16). These are useful research leads, not independently verified datasets. The underlying concern is still substantial: prestigious disabled roles have repeatedly created opportunity for non-disabled performers while disabled performers remain excluded from both disabled and incidental roles.

It still happens with many modern productions casting non-disabled actors, but public opinion is changing, so this could easily shorten shelf-life or lead to damaging publicity.

Disability is always a story, both on screen and behind the camera. There have been attempts to mitigate negative press by giving minor roles to disabled actors to mask the lead disabled role going to a non-disabled actor, but this is an unacceptable practice. In simple terms, surrounding the act of cripping-up with disabled actors in different roles does not make it acceptable.

[A good example of the strength of feeling about cripping-up is the response to The Elephant Man which the BBC announced in 2019](https://www.standard.co.uk/culture/tvfilm/bbc-under-fire-for-disappointing-casting-of-charlie-heaton-over-disabled-actor-for-elephant-man-role-a3923511.html), where there was a backlash from the disabled community about the casting, and then the BBC added fuel to the fire by saying that supporting roles would be cast with disabled actors.

If this mistake is made, consider re-casting the role.

### 3. Treating different lived experiences as interchangeable

Casting a performer with a non-apparent disability does not automatically make them suitable for every apparently disabled role. Authenticity depends on the role's embodiment, language, culture and narrative meaning. Equally, a rigid apparent/non-apparent rule can be too crude: some disabilities fluctuate, some performers mask, and some transpositions are legitimate. Make the decision role by role with relevant lived-experience advice.

It also unnecessarily takes opportunity away from actors who have few other options because of the lack of incidental roles cast as visibly disabled.

### 4. Assuming there are “no disabled actors”

Disabled actors have long worked in stage and screen, including [Esmond Knight](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm0460874/?ref_=fn_all_nme_1) and [Lionel Barrymore](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm0000859/?ref_=tt_cst_t_3). Routes into the profession now include drama schools, disability-led theatre, comedy and other performance disciplines. A failed search may reflect an inaccessible casting process, an unnecessarily narrow brief, weak outreach, inadequate time or poor industry networks—not an absence of talent. *Come As You Are* was among the productions [criticised for non-disabled casting](https://nacdd.org/come-as-you-are-unless-you-are-an-actor-whos-disabled/).

[Jim Sheridan, director of My Left Foot, has even said that he does “not think it’s right anymore” for “able-bodied” actors to play disabled characters,](https://news.sky.com/story/my-left-foot-director-i-would-cast-disabled-actor-in-daniel-day-lewis-role-if-film-was-made-today-12336232) and today he would make very different casting decisions.

### 5. Portrayal of neurodivergence

If a character is explicitly autistic or otherwise neurodivergent, seek relevant neurodivergent talent and expertise. [*Patience*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt31495377/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0_tt_6_nm_2_in_0_q_Patience) cast autistic actor [Ella Maisy Purvis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ella_Maisy_Purvis) as an autistic lead. By contrast, [*Music*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt7541720/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_4_tt_6_nm_1_in_1_q_music) received [substantial criticism of its casting and portrayal](https://time.com/5942094/sia-music-disability-representation/).

“Autistic-coded” is an audience or critical interpretation, not necessarily a diagnosis written into the text. Coding should prompt questions about stereotype, intent and casting, but an AI must not declare either a character or actor autistic without sufficient evidence.

[Vincent D'Onofrio's Wilson Fisk](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt3322312/characters/nm0000352/) has been interpreted by some viewers as neurodivergent-coded. The linked [commentary on the performance](https://www.themarysue.com/fiskal-responsibilty/) is useful critical material, but it does not establish an explicit diagnosis for the character or prove that the casting process was “authentic” in the same sense as an explicitly autistic role.

### 6. Presuming that progressive conditions cannot be portrayed by disabled actors

The casting of [Eddie Redmayne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_Redmayne) as [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking) in [*The Theory of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Everything_(2014_film)) prompted debate about portraying progressive disability. Proposed alternatives included multiple actors or visual effects. The BBC's *Better* later cast disabled actor [Zak Ford-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zak_Ford-Williams) as Owen, who initially masks his cerebral palsy before the character acquires additional impairments following meningitis. [Ford-Williams drew on his own embodied experience](https://www.pressparty.com/pg/newsdesk/BBC1/view/314859/), demonstrating that progressive change can be explored with a disabled performer rather than used as an automatic reason to exclude one.

### 7. Transposing a character's disability

Disability is complex, and literal diagnostic matching is not always necessary. When the story concerns barriers, embodiment or social experience, transposition may preserve the dramatic meaning while aligning the role with the actor. It requires informed creative judgement, consultation and the performer's consent.

In [Gregory Maguire’s novel Wicked, Nessarose is born without arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicked_(Maguire_novel)#Major_characters), but in the movie she is a wheelchair user. Nothing is necessarily lost in the screen interpretation because it was approached creatively and sensitively.

This can also work when it comes to historical figures when their story of being a disabled person is more important than the accuracy of casting a condition.

### 8. Allowing disability to exist without defining the entire character

There are plenty of examples where the only thing a disabled character has is a storyline about disability. While this can be important and impactful, it is good to create disabled characters that have a life outside of their disability.

[Shardlake](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt27390563/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0_tt_3_nm_5_in_0_q_shardlake), starring [Arthur Hughes](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm5168709/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0_tt_0_nm_8_in_0_q_Arthur%2520Hughes), presents a disabled lead whose impairment and the attitudes around him add subtext without exhausting his identity. [Hughes described disability as “the least interesting thing about him”](https://youtu.be/vVdNSwDb9P0?si=iwaaaNN5de2g_una).

[Davros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davros) from *Doctor Who* is a more contested example. His mobility and life-support system, visual impairment, scarring and villainy have generated debate about the disabled-villain trope. The source article's assertion that he disabled himself in order to create the [Daleks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalek) is not sufficiently stable across the programme's changing canon and has been removed as fact. This example should be used to examine stereotype, agency, technology and casting—not as an uncomplicated portrayal success.

### 9. Portraying disability in the past, future and fantasy

If this is a historical role and the aim is to be historically accurate then it is important that the actor plays the condition as it was, using technologies that were available at the time. [***Magnifiers***](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Magnifying_glass&action=edit&section=1#History)***,*** [***walking sticks***](https://www.abilitysuperstore.com/blogs/news/the-history-of-the-walking-stick)***,*** [***crutches***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crutch#History)***,*** [***rudimentary wheelchairs***](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/history-wheelchair)***,*** [***bath chairs***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_chair)***,*** [***sedan chairs***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litter_(vehicle))***, hoists,*** [***ear trumpets***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_trumpet)***,*** [***signing systems***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_sign_language#History_of_known_sign_languages) and more have been around for hundreds of years. After a hunting accident in his early 40s, [Henry VIII was disabled ](https://youtu.be/ZNSlegFUAmI?si=1EXj7GOSmJHCC8Lg)and used walking sticks, an early type of wheelchair and hoists to help with his mobility.

Thinking about how conditions would have presented is an important part of the research of the role, but there are degrees of accuracy that have to be considered. In the Channel 5 drama The Hardacres, the character Harry Hardacre has Cerebral Palsy. [The actor had to imagine what it was like to have had his condition in the late Victorian era](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DBQ7t2GgX9I/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&igsh=MzRlODBiNWFlZA%3D%3D).

There are degrees of accuracy and if the production is set in an alternate past, or has elements of supernatural or other imaginative contexts of storytelling, then there is more artistic licence. In the future or in fantasy then there is no limit to the possibilities. In [*Good Omens*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1869454/?ref_=nm_flmg_job_1_cdt_t_2) the actor [Liz Carr](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm4419317/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0_tt_0_nm_8_in_0_q_liz%2520carr) played the angel Saraqael as a wheelchair user in heaven… why not? Another couple of examples are [Geordie La Forge](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0092455/characters/nm0000996/) in [*Star Trek: The Next Generation*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0092455/) who is blind but has a seeing device that enables him to see the world in ways sighted people don’t, and a final example is [Imperator Furiosa](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1392190/characters/nm0000234/?ref_=tt_cst_c_2) in [*Mad Max: Fury Road*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1392190/), who has a robot arm from a dystopian future.

### 10. Physical action and stunt work

Never assume a disabled actor's physical capabilities or limits. For fighting, running, riding, driving, swimming or dancing, ask about the performer's skills and access requirements just as you would for any other specialist performance.

Disabled actors may have training in stage combat, period dance, movement, disability sport, riding or other disciplines. Ask about relevant skills at the casting stage and describe the action requirements accessibly and accurately.

Body doubling can be difficult when a stunt performer cannot reproduce the actor's movement authentically. Plan additional rehearsal, matching and camera work with the actor, stunt coordinator, access coordinator and relevant specialists. A performer must never be pressured to undertake stunt work in order to protect authenticity or their employment. Apply the same professional risk assessment, informed consent, insurance and stop-work authority used for any stunt, with reasonable adjustments where required.

> “By prioritising disability inclusion, organisations tap into unique skills, perspectives and talents of disabled people, building a more diverse, innovative and inclusive environment within our industry. Look at the word ‘creative’ in creative arts. We are perfectly placed to create environments in which to do our best work. The energy that the presence of disability creates within a production is a resource of creative potential… I feel amidst the advances being made embedding D/deaf, neurodivergent and disability equality within our industry, a disabled person on a stage or screen is still a political act. The presence of marginalised groups can bring about social and political change: the normalised presence of marginalised groups cements it. The rest is celebration.”
>
> — Zak Ford-Williams, actor

## Governance rails for production and AI systems

### Required behaviours

An AI or production workflow applying this guide should:

1. **Start with the story requirement.** Identify what the script actually requires in relation to embodiment, language, culture, history, action and audience understanding.
2. **Challenge the non-disabled default.** Review every role for incidental casting unless a defensible requirement makes that inappropriate.
3. **Separate portrayal from access.** A creative consultant advises on narrative and meaning; an access coordinator supports equitable participation. One person may hold both competencies, but the production must not assume this.
4. **Search accessibly and proportionately.** Use specialist networks, accessible breakdowns, sufficient timescales and open adjustment routes before concluding that talent is unavailable.
5. **Prioritise relevant lived experience.** For embodied, linguistic or culturally specific roles, seek performers whose experience is meaningfully connected to the character.
6. **Pay for expertise.** Treat script, language, cultural, portrayal and access consultancy as professional work.
7. **Record uncertainty.** Label inference, community interpretation and retrospective diagnosis. Do not silently convert them into fact.
8. **Protect sensitive data.** Store adjustment information separately from creative evaluation wherever practicable. Limit access to people who need it operationally.
9. **Test the whole production system.** Evaluate audition, travel, rehearsal, costume, makeup, locations, scheduling, fatigue, communication, intimacy, action, publicity and post-production—not only casting.
10. **Retain human accountability.** The named producer, director, casting director and relevant department leads remain responsible for decisions; an AI recommendation is never approval.

### Prohibited or high-risk behaviours

The system must not:

- diagnose a performer or infer disability from appearance, movement, voice, behaviour, online biography or use of assistive technology;
- rank candidates by perceived “severity”, medical legitimacy or anticipated adjustment cost;
- disclose disability or adjustment information beyond the agreed operational purpose;
- reject a candidate because an adjustment may be needed without lawful, individual and documented consideration;
- assume that one disabled person can authenticate every disability experience;
- use a consultant or minor disabled role to legitimise inauthentic lead casting;
- recommend prosthetics, movement imitation, vocal imitation or simulated sign language without specialist ethical and creative review;
- invent historical diagnoses or present speculative medical claims as fact;
- pressure a performer to expose personal experience, undertake unsafe action or turn lived experience into publicity; or
- claim that representation is successful solely because a disabled performer was cast.

### Human review triggers

Mandatory senior human review is required when:

- a non-disabled performer is proposed for an explicitly disabled role;
- a condition, language or cultural identity is being transposed;
- the production intends to simulate disability through movement, makeup, prosthetics, voice or visual effects;
- a historical diagnosis is disputed or based on retrospective interpretation;
- creative advice from a disabled consultant or performer is rejected;
- disability-related data may influence casting, insurance, scheduling or contractual decisions;
- an adjustment is said to be impracticable;
- a performer is expected to undertake action that materially interacts with their impairment or equipment; or
- publicity may disclose a performer's disability, diagnosis or private experience.

## Production evaluation framework

| Stage | Questions | Evidence to retain |
|---|---|---|
| Development | Is disability explicit, implicit, incidental or absent? What barriers and relationships matter? | Script analysis, source research, consultation brief |
| Writers' room | Were disabled writers or consultants able to influence the work? Were they paid and credited? | Contracts, attendance, decision log, agreed changes |
| Casting brief | Which characteristics are essential, adaptable or irrelevant? Is the language respectful and open? | Approved breakdown, rationale, access statement |
| Search | Were specialist networks and accessible routes used with sufficient time? | Outreach log, agencies contacted, timescales |
| Audition | Were materials, venue, platform and process accessible? | Adjustment record, candidate communications, issue log |
| Decision | Was relevant lived experience considered without diagnosis-by-assumption? | Casting rationale with sensitive data minimised |
| Rehearsal and production | Are access, scheduling, costume, makeup, movement, intimacy and safety integrated? | Access plan, risk assessments, rehearsal notes, change log |
| Edit and post-production | Does framing, sound, music, visual language or editing alter the intended portrayal? | Consultant review, performer feedback where contracted, edit decisions |
| Marketing and publicity | Is disability being sensationalised, disclosed without consent or reduced to inspiration? | Approved language, consent record, accessible publicity assets |
| Release and learning | How did disabled audiences and participants respond? What will change next time? | Feedback, complaints, response actions, lessons learned |

## Minimum decision record

For each disability-related casting or portrayal decision, record:

- production, role and decision owner;
- whether portrayal is explicit, implicit or incidental;
- essential and adaptable characteristics;
- evidence sources and confidence level;
- consultation undertaken and the consultant's remit;
- options considered, including disabled talent;
- access implications and responsible owner;
- performer consent and publicity boundaries;
- final decision and rationale;
- dissenting advice or unresolved uncertainty; and
- review date or trigger.

### Claims requiring current verification

Before the guidance is used for a live production, verify:

- whether every linked resource remains available and current;
- the production's jurisdiction and applicable equality, employment, privacy and safety duties;
- the current wording and status of commissioner or broadcaster commitments;
- whether a named actor has publicly self-identified in the way the production intends to state;
- the current availability and remit of performer directories and specialist organisations;
- all numerical representation, prevalence and awards claims;
- all quotations against the original recording or publication;
- historical claims against reputable scholarship rather than general-reference pages alone; and
- factual claims about a programme's casting process, consultation or creative intent.
